A PROOF OF THE WALLACE-BOLYAI-GERWIEN THEOREM

Ali ADALI

14 July, 2021

Abstract

The Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem states that any two flat shapes of equal area can be cut into pieces and rearranged into each other using only slides and rotations. This work presents a proof.

Let $A$ and $B$ be polygons.

Definition 1.

A simple move is a translation or a rotation of a polygon. If $A$ can be cut into smaller polygons and through a sequence of simple moves the polygons can be arranged to form $B$ we call $B$ is simply obtainable from $A$.

Theorem 2. [Wallace-Bolyai-Gerwien Theorem]

$B$ is simply obtainable from $A$ if and only if $A$ and $B$ have the same area.

Proof.

Simple moves keep the total area unchanged, hence if $B$ is obtainable from $A$ then they must have the same area. This proves the 'only if' part.

To prove the 'if' part we first observe the following properties for any polygons $X$, $Y$ and $Z$:

  1. If $X$ is simply obtainable from $Y$, then $Y$ is simply obtainable from $X$ since any simple move is reversible.
  2. If $X$ is simply obtainable from $Y$ and $Y$ is simply obtainable from $Z$ then $X$ is simply obtainable from $Z$. This is readily obtained from the super partition (i.e., the union of the two partitions).
  3. Any triangle is simply obtainable from a rectangle through the following process: The midpoints of two adjacent sides of the triangle are marked, and two lines passing through these marked points perpendicular to the third side are used as the cutting lines. The little triangles then can be rotated $180$-degrees around the corresponding midpoints to obtain a rectangle.
  4. A rectangle with dimensions $(a,b)$ is simply obtainable from a rectangle with dimensions $(an, b/n)$ for any positive integer $n$. This is obtained by cutting the $b$-side into $n$ equal parts through cuts parallel to the $a$-side and sticking the rectangles one after the other from their $b/n$-sides. An immediate consequence of this property is:
  5. For a positive rational number $r$, a rectangle with dimensions $(a,b)$ is simply obtainable from a rectangle with dimensions $(ar, b/r)$.
  6. A rectangle with dimensions $(a,b)$ is simply obtainable from a rectangle with dimensions $(1,ab)$. To observe this, we first assume that \begin{equation} \label{eq.1} 1 < b^2 < 1+a^2 \end{equation} if not, we begin with simply obtaining a rectangle with dimensions $(a',b') = (ar,b/r)$ from the original rectangle where we choose a rational number $r$ such that $b/r$ is very close to $1$ but slightly larger and work with the rectangle $(a',b')$ instead.
    Denote the vertices of the rectangle by $A, B, C, D$. Since $b > 1$, we mark the unique right triangle $CBH$ where $CH = 1$, $CHB$ is a right angle, and $CBH$ has a common interior with the rectangle $ABCD$. Call $|BH| = x$. The Pythagorean theorem and \eqref{eq.1} imply \[ 1+x^2 = b^2 < 1+ a^2 \] hence $x < a$ and thus $CBH$ entirely lies inside the rectangle $ABCD$ (since otherwise it would imply $x > a$ as $a$ would be a side of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is part of $x$). When the rectangle is cut through the $BH$ line and $CH$ line segment, and the resulting two triangles are translated into their new positions (red to red and blue to blue), the resulting rectangle $CHIG$ has dimensions $(1,ab)$.

As the final step, we prove that for any two polygons $P_1$ and $P_2$, whose areas are $1$, one is simply obtainable from the other. To see this, we begin with dissecting $P_1$ into triangles (which is obviously possible), and we convert each triangle first into a rectangle through property (3) and then each rectangle into a rectangle whose one side is $1$ through property (6). Then we glue each resulting rectangle one under the other from their sides of length $1$. The outcome is a unit square. We can apply a similar process with the polygon $P_2$ and obtain the same unit square. The second process is reversible from property (1). We use property (2) to combine the $P_1$ process with the inverse of the $P_2$ process to simply obtain $P_2$ from $P_1$. This completes the proof for polygons whose areas are $1$, and evidently for all polygons with the same area. $\square$
